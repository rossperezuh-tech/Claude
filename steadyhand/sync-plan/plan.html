Reel 00 — The brief

00:00:00

What the artist actually wants

In plain terms: they want their music played in movies. That's the sync-licensing business — a supervisor drops a track under a scene, the artist earns a fee up front plus performance royalties on the back end, and every placement pulls new listeners toward the catalogue. It's the most durable income a songwriter can build that isn't tied to touring.

Two things win sync placements: music that's technically ready to license (rights clean, stems and instrumentals on hand) and a presentation that makes the emotion obvious fast. The reference — The Space Pit's interactive single — nails the second: it doesn't describe the song, it screens it. We build both halves.

Reel 04 — How music actually gets into movies

00:31:20

The field guide behind the strategy — what makes a song licensable, who to reach, and the deal shape to expect. This is what The Advisor and The Back Office run day to day.

01 Make it clearable

One-stop or it doesn't get used

  • Own or control both sides — master recording and the publishing/composition. A supervisor on a deadline picks the song they can clear with one signature.
  • Have the deliverables ready — WAV master, instrumental, stems, and 15/30/60-second edits.
  • Register everything — with a PRO (ASCAP/BMI), SoundExchange, and the MLC, so back-end royalties actually reach the artist.
  • Tag the metadata — clean titles, writer splits, ISRC, contact — embedded in the files.

02 Know who places it

The people who put songs to picture

  • Music supervisors — film, TV, streaming. The primary target.
  • Sync agencies & libraries — they pitch on the artist's behalf; worth it for reach, costs a split.
  • Trailer houses & ad agencies — fast money, big exposure.
  • Indie films, festivals & student projects — where a newer artist builds real screen credits.

03 Pitch like a supervisor thinks

Short, targeted, feel-first

  • Lead with the link — the cinematic experience, not a paragraph. Let them feel it.
  • Reference the scene — "for a tense night-drive," not "check out my music."
  • One or two tracks, not the catalogue — curated reads as professional.
  • Make yes easy — state that it's one-stop and instantly clearable up front.

04 Understand the deal

So the artist signs from knowledge

  • Two licenses per placement — a sync license (composition) and a master-use license (recording). Owning both means one negotiation.
  • Up-front fee + back-end — a sync fee at signing, plus performance royalties collected by the PRO whenever it airs.
  • Exclusive vs. non-exclusive — non-exclusive keeps a song working in many places; weigh exclusivity only for real money.
  • Watch the terms — media, territory, term, and MFN. This is where The Advisor earns its keep.

Field guide, not legal advice — every placement gets its own contract review before signing.
